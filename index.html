Being Heard

When I arrived at Penn State as a Criminology and Psychology intended double major bound for law school, I already knew that I cared heavily about justice, but I didn't always know how to voice it. Over the past year I have come to understand rhetoric not as persuasion alone, but as the foundations of life. It's how people construct meaning, navigate disagreements, and hold one another accountable all of which I sought to address over the past two semesters.

Through work on topics like eyewtiness testimony, criminal justice reforms, the science of how our memories work, and student wellness, I learned that effective communication isn't just delivering a message. It's recognizing your audience, shaping emotion, and making devicive purposeful choices about how ideas can and are framed. RCL pushed me beyond just "having an opinion" it taught me to build arguments that are thoughtful, ethical, and centered around things that effect ALL OF US.

As a musician and a member of Pride of the Lions Pep band at Penn State I have always valued expression and connection. RCL expanded that understanding beyond music and into civic and social life. The class and the rest of those I have taken this year have taught me that rhetoric is the bridge between individual voice and collective change, and that justice does not just depend on laws, but also on the strength and integrity of the arguments behind them.

PAS 1: Starting Off with a... BOOM!

There is something about Drum Corps that never quite leaves your system. Once a band kid, always a band kid, and I say that with full pride. In this blog thread I will be analyzing DCI (Drum Corps International) winning shows, the kind of performances that leave you with chills, in awe, and occasionally in tears. DCI is essentially the elite league of marching music: top-tier ensembles made up of mostly brass, percussion, and color guard performing incredibly complex shows with Olympic-level precision, beautiful storytelling, and jaw-dropping visuals. If marching band had a Super Bowl, this would be it.

This past DCI season (2025), the Boston Crusaders came out swinging and winning with their 2025 championship show entitled "BOOM." From the very first note to the final rifle toss, the entire performance was an explosion of sounds, colors, and immense energy. What stood out most was that stunning mellophone feature. The Boston Crusaders gave their mello line real moments to shine, with crazy articulated phrases that cut right through the texture of the ensemble. And even more impressive: they all played each other's mellophones. What really pushed "BOOM." over the edge was the overall general effect. Everything from the music to the movements to the emotional arc was all connected into one seamless show.

RCL 4: What Is That in Freedom Units?

At first glance, this shirt just looks like a joke: a classic piece of F1 fan culture featuring Logan Sargeant against an aggressively patriotic background. The text "What the F*** is a Kilometer" screams American pride and ignorance all at once. Underneath the humor, this slogan taps into something deeply woven into our culture: America's ongoing cultural resistance to global norms, and the way nationalism shapes even the most trivial aspects of everyday life, like how we measure our height.

The message is intentionally blunt. It is not trying to argue with data, history, or logic. It is all ethos and emotion. Logically, the slogan does not argue for anything, it does not claim miles are better or the metric system is bad. But it operates as a micro-argument for cultural exceptionalism dressed up in humor. It invites people to laugh with the joke if they share that mindset, or laugh at it if they recognize how ridiculous it sounds. Either way, it sparks a conversation. It condenses a centuries-long clash between global standardization and national pride into six chaotic words.

October 25, 2025

RCL 5: Are You Sure It's a Free Choice?

In his TED Talk, psychological illusionist Derren Brown challenges how we think about belief, decision-making, and free will. The central theme is that human thought and behavior are far more influenced by suggestion and subtle psychological cues than most of us realize. His paradigm shift is a call to move away from the idea that our beliefs and choices are purely self-determined, and to instead recognize the fragile, constructed nature of human perception.

Through live demonstrations, Brown shows how participants' memories and decisions can be manipulated without them realizing it, revealing just how limited our conscious control can be. This completely reframed my understanding of belief systems. This talk is also a masterclass in presentation skills. His style is confident yet conversational, and his pacing keeps the audience hooked through curiosity and suspense. He relies on live psychological demonstrations rather than slides or data points, making the presentation feel like a shared experience rather than a lecture.

PiP 2: Expectations Matter

We are talking about expectations today. Not the ones you put on yourself at 2 a.m., but the ones society hands you the moment you walk into the workplace and exist as a woman. That unspoken expectation of "of course you'll do that." The kind that will not show up in the job description but will definitely shape your entire shift.

When I worked at Express, these expectations were subtle but non-stop. I was a manager, a position I had to work 10x harder for than any man there, but my daily tasks were often associate-level work: folding clothes, cleaning displays, organizing fitting rooms. Meanwhile, the men were more often asked to handle anything that looked authoritative or required physical strength. None of this was stated policy, but it happened often enough to feel normal. What really made it apparent was how customers interacted with us. Despite being a manager, customers regularly looked past me to ask male employees about policies and decisions I was responsible for. It was not hostility. It was assumption. And that is the whole point: misogyny in the workplace does not always look like open discrimination. It often looks like assumptions, habits, and who people instinctively trust.

February 13, 2026

PiP 4: How (Bio)LOGICAL Is It???

We like to say everyone is treated equally, that justice is blind, and that who you are should not matter. But in reality, how you look, what your body shape is, even your genetics often shape how people interpret your behavior before you ever say a word. Biology has become less of a scientific topic and more of a social filter.

Two people can display the exact same behavior, and it is not interpreted the same way. One person is seen as dangerous. Another as troubled. One gets punishment, another gets treatment. Because biology does not exist in a vacuum. It is interpreted through social perceptions like appearance, race, and gender. This matters deeply in the criminal justice system. What starts as a biological difference becomes a social outcome. Biology itself is neutral. A brain injury, hormone levels, a genetic predisposition are simply facts. What is not neutral is the meaning we attach to them. When those meanings vary across groups, biology stops being purely biological and starts becoming political. Biology shapes behavior, but society shapes consequences.

March 28, 2026

WiP 3: The New Age of Artificial Intelligence

The United States Congress should pass federal legislation regulating the use of artificial intelligence in the legal system by requiring transparency in AI tools and establishing clear standards for admissible digital evidence. The regulation of AI in the legal system is an increasingly urgent issue as technology advances faster than the laws designed to govern it. Without proper regulation, AI tools may contribute to privacy violations, wrongful convictions, and the use of unreliable evidence like deepfakes.

I am particularly interested in this topic because I plan to pursue a career in law, and I want to understand how emerging technologies will shape the future of the justice system. AI is already being integrated into legal processes, from predictive policing to evidence analysis, yet there is a lack of consistent standards and oversight. One major misconception about AI in the legal system is that it is inherently objective and unbiased. In reality, AI systems are only as reliable as the data they are trained on, and they can reinforce existing biases or produce inaccurate results.

April 16, 2026

WiP 5: Photos or It Didn't Happen

For my Advocacy Project, I chose to focus on the mandatory recording of all police interrogations and encounters, specifically advocating for policies that require continuous, start-to-finish recording. This cause is particularly important because it directly impacts the fairness and transparency of the legal system. Without clear, objective evidence of what happens during police interactions, situations can quickly become a matter of one person's word against another's, opening the door to misunderstanding, misinterpretation, and serious injustices like false confessions or wrongful convictions.

Recording interrogations and encounters helps address these issues by providing an unbiased, factual account of what actually occurred. It protects individuals from coercion during questioning, while also protecting law enforcement from false accusations. It is not about targeting police. It is about strengthening trust, accountability, and accuracy within our system as a whole. By promoting mandatory recording policies, I hope to emphasize that justice is not just about court outcomes but about ensuring the entire process is fair, transparent, and as trustworthy as possible.

Justice... or Just a Really Good Illusion?

Two posts from a five-part series examining eyewitness testimony, jury bias, and the gaps in our legal system.

⌄

April 3, 2026

CI 3: I Saw It with My Own Eyes... But Did You?

Let me ask you a question: if someone says "I saw them do it," would you believe them? Most people would instinctively say yes. After all, seeing is believing, right? Eyewitness testimony has long been considered one of the most powerful forms of evidence in the American legal system. But the reality is that human memory is far from perfect. It is surprisingly fragile and easily influenced.

Memory does not work like a video recording. It is more like a reconstruction. Every time we recall an event, our brain is actively piecing it back together, and that process can be affected by stress, time, and outside influences. In high-stress situations like witnessing a crime, your brain is not focused on capturing perfect details. It is focused on survival. On top of that, memory can be influenced after the event has occurred. Leading questions, conversations with other witnesses, or media coverage can unintentionally alter how someone remembers what they saw. A confident witness can be incredibly persuasive to a jury, even if their memory is inaccurate. Confidence does not equal accuracy. But in a trial setting, it can be very hard to tell the difference.

April 16, 2026

CI 5: Justice... or Is It Something We Are Still Working Toward?

Over the past few weeks we have unpacked a lot together. We talked about the justice gap and how access to legal representation can completely shape someone's outcome. We looked at eyewitness testimony and how something that feels so reliable can actually be deeply flawed. Then we explored juries and how even well-meaning people carry unconscious biases into the courtroom. So now I want to ask a bigger question: when you put all these pieces together, how fair is the system really?

Imagine someone who cannot afford strong legal representation. Now add inaccurate eyewitness testimony delivered confidently to a jury. Then place that case in front of jurors who, without even realizing it, interpret the defendant's appearance through their own biases. Individually, each of these factors is concerning. Together? Overwhelming. The justice system is often presented as something objective, rooted in facts and fairness. But in reality it is built and carried out by people. And people, no matter how well-intentioned, are imperfect. What matters most is awareness. Once we recognize these patterns we can begin to ask better questions. Justice is not just about what happens in a courtroom. It is also about how we, as a society, understand and engage with the system itself.
